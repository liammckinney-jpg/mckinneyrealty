Chapter One

What You’ve Built

The quiet asset

Somewhere in your files is a closing statement with a date on it — fifteen, twenty-five, maybe forty years ago — recording the day you bought a building that has since done something remarkable, mostly without ceremony. It collected rent. It paid down its own mortgage. It sheltered its income while its value compounded. It may now be worth several times what you paid for it, and it almost certainly represents one of the largest positions on your family’s balance sheet.

Buildings like yours rarely make headlines, and their owners rarely advertise. But the arithmetic is not a secret to everyone: the unsolicited calls, the letters from brokers, the “buyer with strong interest in your area” — you have likely had them for years, and they have likely gotten more frequent. There is a reason for that, and it is worth understanding on your own terms rather than a caller’s.

This guide exists to put a framework around the decisions that eventually arrive with a long-held building: whether to keep holding, whether to refinance, whether to sell, and how — in any of those cases — the asset moves to the next generation without being diminished in transit. It does not conclude that you should sell. It does not conclude that you should hold. It concludes that owners who understand their position make better decisions than owners who respond to phone calls — and it is written to move you firmly into the first group.

Why the calls keep coming

Understand what you own in today’s terms. A stabilized, income-producing apartment building in an Ontario market has become a peculiar kind of asset: expensive to replicate and increasingly rare in prospect. The past two years delivered record purpose-built rental construction across the country — and the pipeline behind that surge is now visibly thinning, with national housing starts projected to decline each year through 2028 as construction costs, development charges, and financing conditions squeeze project feasibility. New buildings must lease at today’s costs; yours operates at yesterday’s basis. Analysts covering the Ontario market expect new supply to fall off materially within two to three years.

Institutional capital has noticed. Institutions deployed nearly $15 billion into Canadian real estate in 2025 — roughly a third of the entire investment market and their largest share of acquisitions since 2021 — and multifamily investment outpaced every other asset class in the final quarter of the year. Transaction activity in the GTA opened 2026 up sharply from the year before. None of this obligates you to do anything. All of it explains your phone.

What this guide covers

Chapter 2 lays out the economics of continuing to hold — costs and value, both columns, no thumb on the scale. Chapter 3 presents the framework at the centre of this guide: hold, refinance, or sell as a structured decision rather than a reaction, with an interactive tool for the arithmetic. Chapter 4 explains why your tax position — not your asking price — is the first number that matters, and why that conversation precedes any listing conversation by months or years. Chapter 5 addresses succession: how a building crosses a generation without a forced sale. Chapter 6 covers what a properly run disposition involves, for the owner who chooses one. Chapter 7 closes where our own family’s story does — with what stewardship across generations actually looks like.

You have already done the hard part. You bought well, or held long enough that it no longer matters, and you let the asset work. What remains is to finish the job with the same discipline it started with.

Chapter Two

The Economics of Holding

Both columns

Any adviser who tells a long-hold owner that the decision is obvious — in either direction — is selling something. The truthful version is a ledger with two columns, and it is worth writing both of them down without flinching.

The cost column

Operating costs have outpaced regulated revenue. Insurance has repriced to a permanently higher base over the past decade; renewals have recently cooled. Utilities, maintenance labour, and materials have all repriced. Meanwhile, for buildings first occupied before November 2018, revenue growth on sitting tenancies is bounded by Ontario’s annual guideline — with above-guideline increases available only through a formal application process with its own costs, timelines, and limits. The squeeze between market-priced expenses and regulated revenue is real, and it compounds.

The capital-needs curve bends upward. Buildings age on a schedule that does not consult their owners. Roofs, boilers, windows, balconies, electrical services — and, for mid-rise stock, elevators, where modernization is routinely a six-figure event. A building held for decades is often approaching several of these at once. The honest question is not whether the building has been maintained — it is what the next ten years of capital work costs, and who should be the one to fund it.

The regulatory workload is heavier than it was. Landlord and Tenant Board timelines, documentation standards, and compliance obligations have made self-management more demanding than the era in which many long-hold owners learned the trade. For some families this is a manageable evolution; for others it is the quiet reason ownership has stopped being satisfying.

The near-term market is softer than the headlines of three years ago. Record recent completions pushed national purpose-built vacancy to 3.1 percent — up from 2.2 percent a year earlier — and rent growth has moderated, with CMHC expecting further easing in the near term as new units absorb. An owner underwriting the next few years should do so with those facts, not with 2022's.

The value column

Scarcity is moving in your favour. The same data has a second half: the construction pipeline behind those record completions is contracting, with national starts projected to decline each year through 2028 and analysts expecting Ontario’s new supply to fall off materially within two to three years. Stabilized existing stock — your column of the ledger — becomes relatively scarcer as that happens. The near-term soft patch and the medium-term scarcity are both true. They are about different years.

Your building carries embedded upside no new building has. Long tenancies at below-market rents are, viewed as an investor views them, purchasable future income — loss-to-lease, in underwriting language. Time closes that gap on its own schedule. The patient holder is positioned to receive it; equally, it is a significant part of what a buyer is paying for, which matters in Chapter 6.

Refinance capacity is stored liquidity. Decades of amortization and appreciation mean the building can likely return substantial capital to your hands through refinancing — without a sale, and therefore without a taxable event. Chapter 3 treats this as the underused middle option it is.

The income is durable. People need housing in every part of the cycle. Whatever else is true of the cost column, few assets your family could own produce income with this reliability, secured by something this tangible.

What the ledger is for

The ledger does not produce a verdict; it produces clarity about which column is growing faster for your specific building and your specific family. A well-maintained building with modest coming capex, engaged successors, and comfortable financing reads one way. A building facing an elevator, a roof, and a generation with no interest in operating it reads another. The next chapter turns that reading into a decision framework.

Chapter Three

Hold, Refinance, or Sell: A Framework

Three legitimate answers

Most owners are only ever presented with two options, by people who earn a fee on exactly one of them. There are three, and every one of them is, in the right circumstances, the correct answer.

Holding is the default that built the asset, and it remains correct when the income still serves its purpose, the family still wants the work, and the capital-needs curve is fundable from operations. Nothing in this guide argues with forty years of evidence that patient holding works.

Refinancing is the under-discussed middle. A building with decades of accumulated equity can return a substantial portion of it through a new financing — capital to hand, no sale, no taxable event — while the building continues operating. Families use refinance proceeds to fund capital work without draining savings, to equalize among children where one will operate and others will not, or to diversify a concentrated balance sheet. It restores leverage, and leverage restored is risk restored — the same conservatism that governed the purchase must govern the refinancing. But for the owner whose reservations are about liquidity or concentration rather than about ownership itself, this is frequently the answer that fits.

And refinancing has a second form worth naming on its own: refinance and grow. The equity in a long-held building is not only stored liquidity — it is a down payment on the next one. An owner who refinances and acquires again is running the exact discipline that built most of this province’s quiet multifamily fortunes, described from the buyer’s side in Volume I of this series: equity redeployed instead of consumed, the tax never crystallizing because the sale never happens. For families with engaged successors, it is often the most elegant succession move available — the next generation cuts its teeth operating a building purchased with the first building’s equity, learning the trade on an asset the family controls rather than inheriting a decision they never practiced for. Growth is not every steward’s answer; an owner already stretched by one building’s capital needs should not borrow into two. But for the family whose hesitation is “we’re not done building” rather than “we’re done owning,” this is the option the cold-callers will never mention — because there is no listing in it.

Selling is correct when the ledger says so: when the coming capital program belongs to the next owner, when no successor wants the operation, when the family’s capital has a genuinely better home, when concentration risk has become uncomfortable, or when the owner is simply done — a reason that requires no further justification after decades of stewardship. A sale executed deliberately, with the tax position engineered in advance and the asset positioned properly, is not the end of stewardship. It is its final act, done well or done poorly.

The questions that sort them

Five questions do most of the sorting work. What is the capital for — income, a project, the next generation, peace of mind? Who operates the building in ten years — and do they want to? What does the next decade of capital work cost, and who should fund it? What is the tax position today — and what would it be after the structuring moves of Chapters 4 and 5? And what does the arithmetic actually say — not felt, but computed?

The first four are conversations. The fifth is a calculation, and it should be done before any of the conversations harden into positions.

A framework, not a forecast

Notice what this chapter has not done: it has not told you what the market will do, and it has not told you which door to walk through. Markets reprice; frameworks hold. An owner who can articulate which of the three answers fits — and why, in terms of the five questions — is no longer a name on a cold-caller’s list. They are a principal with a plan, and every subsequent conversation, including any conversation with us, goes better because of it.

Chapter Four

Your Tax Position Comes First

The number on the cheque

Every owner knows their building’s approximate value. Very few know their approximate net proceeds — and the distance between those two numbers is where most disposition planning fails before it begins.

A sale price is a headline. The cheque is what remains after a stack of deductions that has been quietly assembling for as long as you have owned the building: the mortgage discharge, including any prepayment penalty if the sale lands mid-term; transaction costs; and then the tax components — the recapture of every dollar of capital cost allowance claimed across the hold, and the capital gain on the appreciation itself, both crystallizing in a single tax year. For a building held for decades with CCA claimed throughout, the combined liability is routinely the largest single line in the stack, and it arrives all at once.

None of this is a reason not to sell. Decades of tax deferral had real value — that was the plan working, not failing. It is a reason to know the number before forming a view on price, because owners anchor on the headline and are ambushed by the cheque, and ambushed owners make poor decisions late in a process when their leverage is lowest.

The sequence most owners get backwards

The conventional sequence is: decide to sell, call a broker, list, and let the accountant sort out the taxes at filing time. That sequence forfeits money, and sometimes a great deal of it, because the most valuable tax planning happens before a transaction exists.

How the building is held determines what is possible. A personally held building and a corporately held one face different mechanics at sale. A corporately held building opens a question that a personal holding cannot: whether the transaction is a sale of the asset or a sale of the shares — structures with genuinely different tax outcomes for the seller, different risk profiles for the buyer, and therefore different prices at which each side should rationally transact. Owners who learn this vocabulary before negotiating have options; owners who learn it during diligence have regrets.

And Canada offers no escape hatch on a plain sale. American owners can defer through a like-kind exchange; a Canadian sale is a taxable event, full stop. What Canadian law offers instead are structuring tools — corporate rollovers, reorganizations, and the estate mechanisms of the next chapter — and nearly all of them share one property: they work when arranged in advance, and poorly or not at all when attempted mid-transaction.

The two-conversation rule

The practical discipline is simple: the accountant conversation precedes the broker conversation, ideally by a year or more. Bring your accountant the building’s cost base, the CCA history, the corporate structure if there is one, and the honest range of family intentions — hold, refinance, sell, transfer. Ask what each path costs in tax as things stand, and what advance structuring would change. Then, and only then, is a conversation about market value standing on solid ground.

We hold this position knowing it delays our own involvement, and we hold it anyway, because the alternative produces worse outcomes for owners. When McKinney Multifamily Group conducts a pre-sale consultation, the tax position is where it starts — not because we advise on tax, which we do not, but because no sound disposition strategy can be built without it.

Chapter Five

Succession Without a Forced Sale

The transfer is the point

If generational wealth means anything, it means the asset outlives its first owner without being broken on the way through. Yet that passage — not vacancy, not interest rates — is where long-held buildings are most often lost, because tax law treats death as a disposition: absent planning, the building is deemed sold at fair market value, the accumulated gain and recapture crystallize, and the estate must find the tax from somewhere. When “somewhere” turns out to be a hurried sale of the building itself, decades of stewardship end at the worst table an owner’s family will ever negotiate at: a deadline, a liability, and a buyer who knows both.

Planned succession exists to make sure that table is never set.

The vocabulary of a planned transfer

What follows is a map of the standard mechanisms — awareness-level, deliberately so. Each is a question for your accountant and your lawyer, and the right combination depends entirely on your family’s facts.

The estate freeze. The cornerstone concept of Canadian succession planning: the current owner’s interest is fixed — frozen — at today’s value, while future growth accrues to the next generation, typically through new shares. The founder’s eventual tax liability becomes a known, plannable quantity instead of an open-ended one that grows with every year of appreciation; the growth from freeze-day forward is taxed a generation later.

The Section 85 rollover. The mechanism that moves a personally held building into a corporation without triggering immediate tax — frequently the enabling first step, because most of the flexible succession structures require corporate ownership to exist at all. This is also why Volume I of this series urges buyers to settle structure before their first offer: options at the end of a hold are largely determined at its beginning. If your building is personally held today, this is the first question to bring to your accountant — the answer shapes everything after it.

Family trusts and the 21-year rule. Trusts can hold growth shares, spread outcomes across children and grandchildren, and postpone hard allocation decisions. They come with their own clock: Canadian tax law deems a trust to dispose of its assets every 21 years, a horizon that must be planned for from the day the trust is settled, not discovered in year twenty.

Insurance as liquidity. Where a terminal tax liability cannot be structured away, families frequently fund it in advance — life insurance sized to the projected liability, so the estate pays the tax without selling the asset. Not a tax strategy; a liquidity strategy. Often the difference between a building that stays and a building that goes.

The part the professionals cannot do

The mechanisms are the lawyers’ and accountants’ craft. What only the family can supply is the governance: who operates the building, who merely benefits from it, and how the two are kept from becoming the same argument every Thanksgiving. The most elegant freeze fails if no one wants to run the asset, and a modest structure succeeds if the operating question was answered honestly. Some families answer it by growing: a second building, purchased with the first one’s refinanced equity and operated by the successor, turns succession from a paperwork event into an apprenticeship — the next generation proves the operating answer years before the transfer depends on it. In our experience the families who transfer buildings well are not the ones with the most sophisticated structures — they are the ones who had the operating conversation early, out loud, and revisited it as children became adults.

Succession planning is where holding and selling stop being opposites: a family that has built the structure to hold across a generation has also, necessarily, built the clarity to sell well if it ever chooses to. The next chapter is for the owner who does.

Chapter Six

If You Sell: Maximizing the Outcome

Deliberate, not reactive

There are two kinds of dispositions: the ones that begin with a plan, and the ones that begin with a phone call. The entire difference in outcome — and it is routinely a difference measured in six figures — lies in which one yours is.

A reactive sale accepts a buyer’s timing, a buyer’s information advantage, and a buyer’s framing of value. A deliberate sale reverses all three. Everything in this chapter is about that reversal, at the level of principle; the operational companion — our Disposition Readiness Checklist and the full disposition process — is the client deliverable McKinney Multifamily Group brings to an engagement, and this guide will not counterfeit it in summary form.

Preparation is pricing

By the time a building is on the market, most of its price has already been determined — by the state of its records. Multifamily assets are bought on their numbers, and numbers that cannot be verified are numbers a buyer discounts. Clean financial statements that reconcile; a current, accurate rent roll; organized tenancy files; utility and tax histories; documentation of capital work — these are not administrative niceties. They are the difference between a buyer underwriting your building’s actual income and a buyer underwriting their own skepticism.

The same is true of the physical asset, with a discipline worth stating plainly: the goal is not renovation, it is the removal of excuses. Deferred items that are cheap to fix and expensive to negotiate — the leak stain, the dead intercom, the mechanical room that photographs like a warning — get resolved. Major capital items get honest engineering assessments, because a priced, documented liability negotiates better than a discovered one. Buyers pay for certainty. Preparation manufactures it.

Positioning is the second price

The building sold as a listing and the building sold as an investment are the same building at different prices. An investment-grade process presents an underwritten package — normalized operating statement, documented upside, the loss-to-lease story told with evidence — to a curated universe of qualified purchasers, and runs a structured process that lets competition, not a single caller’s opening number, discover the price. Your decades of below-market tenancies, properly documented, are a purchasable asset to the right buyer; unexplained, they are a reason to bid low.

Discretion deserves its own word. Many owners have excellent reasons — tenants, staff, family, competitors — to prefer that a sale not be public. An off-market process, run properly against a known buyer pool, trades some breadth for confidentiality while preserving competitive tension. It is a legitimate strategy, not a lesser one, and the current market has depth enough to support it: transaction activity has returned decisively, with GTA multifamily volume opening 2026 sharply above the prior year. That is stated as a fact about liquidity, not an argument about timing — the framework of Chapter 3, not the market’s mood, decides whether you sell.

The seat we take

In a disposition engagement, McKinney Multifamily Group’s role is the one this guide has been describing throughout: the tax conversation first, the readiness work before the market ever hears the building’s address, the underwritten positioning, the curated process — on-market or off — and principal-led negotiation through to close. Two Brokers, one accountable relationship, and a standard set by the fact that we advise sellers the way our own family would expect to be advised. Chapter Seven

Stewardship Across Generations

What a held portfolio becomes

Volume I of this series ends with an arithmetic illustration: one building, ordinary assumptions, twenty patient years, and equity several times the original investment. This volume can end with something better than an illustration.

The original McKinney Realty was founded by the family’s first Broker — Sean’s father, Liam’s grandfather — who, alongside the brokerage, quietly practiced everything these two guides describe: he bought an apartment building, held it, refinanced it, and bought the next, until one building had become a portfolio. The portfolio was held. It crossed a generation. Sean carried the practice forward, purchased the local RE/MAX franchise, and grew it into the 75-plus-agent brokerage it is today. Liam is the third generation.

We advise on this work because our family has lived it — slowly, unevenly, and across three generations, which is how compounding actually happens. We have sat on your side of the decisions in this guide: the capex years, the refinancing conversations, the succession questions asked around an actual kitchen table. The name over the door has changed; the practice under it has not. We know first-hand what one well-held property can become — and what it takes to keep it whole on the way through.

The steward’s definition of success

Success, for the steward, is not a number. It is optionality preserved: a building whose records would survive diligence tomorrow, a tax position known rather than feared, a structure that permits transfer without forcing sale, a family that has had the operating conversation, and a decision — hold, refinance, sell, or pass on — made from the framework of Chapter 3 rather than from a stranger’s phone call. An owner in that position wins on every path, including the paths not taken.

Getting there is not a transaction. It is a sequence of unhurried conversations — accountant, lawyer, family, and an advisor who understands the asset class — most of which cost nothing but candour, and all of which are worth more the earlier they happen.

Where this guide ends

If this volume has done its work, you now hold a ledger, a framework, and a vocabulary — and the next step is not a listing agreement. It is a conversation about your building, your structure, and your family’s intentions, with no conclusion presumed.
