### Chapter One — Why Multi-Family Compounds

#### The asset class professionals overlook

Most successful professionals and business owners hold their wealth in three places: their business or practice, their principal residence, and a portfolio of public securities. Each has a role. None of them does what a well-financed apartment building does.

A multi-family building is the only widely accessible asset that combines four properties at once: it produces income, it appreciates, it can be financed with long-term insured debt at loan-to-value ratios no other commercial asset class receives, and its debt is retired by the people who live in it. Any one of these characteristics is available elsewhere. The combination is not.

This is not an argument that multi-family outperforms every other asset in every period. It is an argument about mechanism. Public equities compound through retained earnings you never see and prices you don't control. A business compounds through your own continued effort. An apartment building compounds through four engines running simultaneously — and three of them run whether you are paying attention or not.

#### Four engines, one building

**Principal paydown.** Every mortgage payment on the building is funded by rental income and a portion of every payment retires debt. On a multi-million-dollar loan, this is not a rounding error — it is tens of thousands of dollars of equity per year, transferred to your balance sheet by the operation of the asset itself. Owners sometimes call this tenant-funded savings. The description is accurate.

**Cash yield.** After operating expenses and debt service, a soundly underwritten building produces distributable cash. In the early years of a leveraged hold this number is modest by design — leverage trades current yield for equity growth. It rises as rents grow against a fixed debt payment.

**Income growth.** Apartment revenue grows with the rental market, and in Ontario's secondary markets the fundamentals behind that growth are structural: durable rental demand, tight conditions in several key centres, and a forward construction pipeline that is now thinning while the province's housing need is not. Because income-producing property is valued as a multiple of its net operating income, income growth is value growth. A building that earns more is worth more, mechanically.

**Leverage.** This is the multiplier on the first three. When 70 to 85 percent of a building's cost is financed, every point of value growth and every dollar of principal paydown accrues to the 15 to 30 percent you invested. Leverage is also the engine that demands the most respect — it amplifies discipline and indiscipline equally, which is why Chapter 4 of this guide is about underwriting and Chapter 3 is about the governing constraints lenders impose. Used within those constraints, insured leverage is the single largest structural advantage this asset class offers a Canadian investor.

#### One building, one year

Consider an illustration. The figures are deliberately round, the assumptions deliberately ordinary — this is arithmetic, not a forecast, and no outcome is assured.

A 15-unit building in a secondary Ontario market is purchased for $3,000,000 — $200,000 per unit, squarely within the range these markets have recently traded. It is financed with an insured loan at 75 percent of value: $2,250,000 of debt, $750,000 of investor equity. The building produces $142,500 of net operating income. Debt service on a 30-year amortization consumes roughly $129,000 in the first year — of which approximately $39,000 retires principal.

Now hold the building for one unremarkable year, in which net operating income grows a modest 2.5 percent and the market's pricing of that income stands still.

Two things about this illustration matter more than the number itself. First, the result is driven by structure, not heroics — ordinary assumptions produce it because four engines are running at once. Second, compounding at rates like this does not need many years to produce outcomes that look extraordinary in hindsight. That arithmetic — and what disciplined owners do with it across decades — is where this guide ends, in Chapter 7.

It is also, candidly, where most presentations of this asset class go wrong: the arithmetic gets promoted into a promise. It is not one. Rates rise, vacancies happen, expenses surprise, and leverage cuts both ways. What the arithmetic establishes is the *mechanism* — and the case for approaching it with institutional discipline rather than enthusiasm.

#### Why "generational" is the right word

Generational wealth is not a synonym for "a lot of money." It describes wealth with three specific properties: it produces income without consuming the asset, it can be held and managed across a transfer of ownership, and it tends to become more valuable — not less — with time and scarcity.

An apartment building in a supply-constrained market meets all three. It is why so much quietly held family wealth in this province sits in unremarkable brick buildings on secondary-market streets, owned by families who bought one building decades ago, financed it sensibly, and let the engines run. The buildings rarely make headlines. The balance sheets they built are another matter.

The professionals best positioned to follow that path are the ones reading this guide: established income, access to capital, a long horizon, and no need for the building to replace a salary in year one. Multi-family rewards exactly that profile.

#### What this guide covers

The chapters ahead move from thesis to discipline. Chapter 2 makes the case for Ontario's secondary markets with current data. Chapter 3 explains the financing structure — including CMHC's MLI Select program — that makes the arithmetic above possible, and puts an interactive modeling tool in your hands. Chapter 4 teaches you to read a deal the way an underwriter does. Chapter 5 addresses the ownership-structure decisions that must precede your first offer. Chapter 6 maps the path to a first acquisition and the team it requires. Chapter 7 returns to compounding — and to what building across generations actually looks like.

None of it is complicated. All of it rewards being done properly, with advisors who do it as a specialization rather than a sideline. That is the standard this guide is written to.

### Chapter Two — The Ontario Opportunity

#### Two markets, one province

When investors say "Ontario multi-family," most of them mean Toronto. The data argues for looking further.

In the GTA, apartment buildings have traded at an average of roughly $290,000 to $310,000 per unit over the past year. In the secondary markets that ring the province — Kingston, Belleville, Peterborough, Barrie, Hamilton's periphery, the corridor communities — comparable stabilized product trades between $150,000 and $250,000 per unit. The rent gap between these markets is meaningful; the price gap is larger. That asymmetry is the entire secondary-market thesis in one sentence: the investor buys more income per dollar of capital, in markets where the fundamentals are frequently *tighter* than the GTA's, not looser.

Yield spreads tell the same story. Secondary Ontario markets have offered on the order of 50 to 100 basis points of cap-rate premium over comparable GTA assets — with less exposure to condo shadow supply and less competition from the institutional capital that concentrates where deal sizes justify its overhead.

The yield math is obvious. The access is what's scarce. That distinction matters, and Chapter 6 returns to it.

#### Fundamentals, not narratives

Theses are cheap; occupancy is not. The vacancy and rent picture across Ontario's key markets, per CMHC's most recent annual Rental Market Survey:

| Market | Vacancy | Average Rent |
|---|---|---|
| Kingston | 2.4% | $1,713 |
| Belleville | 3.3% | $1,447 |
| Hamilton | 3.5% | $1,552 |
| Barrie | 4.2% | $1,697 |
| GTA | 3.0% | $2,034 (avg. 2BR) |

Two observations. First, several secondary markets run *tighter* than Toronto — Kingston's vacancy describes a market where a vacant unit is a brief event, not a carrying cost. Second, the rent levels leave structural headroom: where in-place rents in older buildings sit well below these market averages, the gap between the two is embedded, purchasable upside. Chapter 4 treats that gap — loss-to-lease — as the underwriting concept it is.

#### The supply story

The most important fact about Ontario's rental market is what happens to supply from here.

Be precise about the present first. 2025 delivered record purpose-built rental construction across most major Canadian centres, and those completions pushed national purpose-built vacancy to 3.1 percent — up from 2.2 percent a year earlier. Renters gained options and rent growth moderated. That is the soft patch the headlines describe, and this guide will not pretend otherwise.

Now look forward, because a building is bought for its next twenty years, not its next four quarters. National housing starts are projected to fall from 259,000 in 2025 to roughly 247,000 this year, 223,000 in 2027, and 216,000 in 2028, as construction costs, development charges, and financing conditions compress project feasibility — and rental starts themselves have now turned down. The units completing today were started in a different environment; the pipeline behind them is thinning. Analysts covering the GTA market expect new supply to fall off materially within two to three years, with upward pressure on rents to follow. A stabilized building purchased through this soft patch is owned into that falloff. In plain terms: the patient buyer acquires near the moment supply peaks and holds into the years it recedes.

Institutional capital has read the same arithmetic. Institutions — pension funds, REITs, private equity, and foreign capital — deployed nearly $15 billion into Canadian real estate in 2025, roughly a third of the entire investment market and their largest share of acquisitions since 2021, and multi-family investment outpaced every other asset class in the final quarter of the year. That capital concentrates, as it always does, at scales where its overhead works. The 4-to-40-unit mid-market remains a private buyer's field. It will not necessarily remain one.

#### Where the buildings actually are

The institutional firms that dominate headlines compete for assets at scales where their cost structures work — and largely ignore everything below their minimums. The result is a structural gap: buildings of roughly four to forty units make up the overwhelming majority of Ontario's secondary-market inventory — on the order of 90 percent — and are served neither by institutional brokerages that fly in for nine-figure mandates nor, adequately, by residential agents for whom an apartment building is an occasional novelty.

That gap is where this guide's readers will transact, and it is the work McKinney Realty was built for. The buildings are there. The sellers are there — many of them the long-hold families described in Chapter 1, now a generation older. What the segment lacks is not opportunity but specialized, principal-led representation on both sides of the table.

### Chapter Three — Financing as the Multiplier

#### The Canadian advantage

Chapter 1 called insured leverage the single largest structural advantage this asset class offers a Canadian investor. Here is why that is not an overstatement.

In most of commercial real estate, debt is conservative, short, and recourse-heavy: office, retail, and industrial buyers routinely finance 55 to 65 percent of value on terms that reprice within five years. Multi-family alone enjoys a federally backstopped insurance regime — through Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation — that exists because rental housing is public policy, not merely private investment. CMHC-insured apartment loans reach loan-to-value ratios, amortization lengths, and interest-rate pricing that no other commercial asset class receives.

The practical consequence: the same dollar of investor equity controls more building, retires more principal, and compounds faster in multi-family than anywhere else on the commercial landscape — legally, structurally, and by design.

#### MLI Select, at altitude

CMHC's flagship program for this purpose is MLI Select. Its mechanics reward buildings — and buyers — that advance three public-policy outcomes: affordability, energy efficiency, and accessibility. Commitments across these categories earn points, and higher point tiers unlock progressively stronger financing: higher maximum leverage, longer amortizations, and reduced insurance premium costs.

The details of the point grid change as the program evolves, which is precisely why this guide does not reproduce them. What matters at thesis level is the shape of the bargain: an investor willing to operate a building in line with these outcomes can access leverage and amortization terms that transform the arithmetic of Chapter 1 — in some configurations dramatically so. A building that qualifies at the highest tiers can be financed with a fraction of the equity a conventional commercial loan would demand, with debt service spread across an amortization measured in multiple decades.

Program parameters, current as of today, live in our Financing Modeler — the interactive tool at the end of this chapter — where they are maintained against CMHC's published grid rather than frozen on a printed page.

#### Amortization as forced savings

Two lender concepts govern everything in this chapter, and both reward being understood in plain language.

**Amortization** is the schedule over which the loan retires. The longer the amortization, the lower each payment — and the more of the building's income remains as cash flow. But every payment, long schedule or short, contains a principal component: the tenant-funded savings of Chapter 1. Extended amortizations do not eliminate paydown; they moderate its early pace in exchange for durability of cash flow, which is frequently the correct trade for a long-horizon holder.

**Debt service coverage ratio (DSCR)** is the lender's governing constraint: the building's net operating income divided by its annual debt payments. A DSCR floor — say, 1.10 — means the building must earn at least $1.10 for every $1.00 it owes the lender annually. In practice, DSCR, not loan-to-value, is what limits most apartment loans. It is the discipline built into the system: the building must carry itself, with margin, before the lender will fund it. An investor who understands DSCR understands why conservative underwriting in Chapter 4 is not caution for its own sake — it is what the financing itself requires.

### Chapter Four — Reading a Deal

#### NOI is the truth

Every building for sale arrives with a story, and the story is usually a pro forma. The discipline of underwriting is the refusal to buy the story.

Net operating income — revenue actually collected, less the expenses actually required to operate the building — is the number from which everything else derives: the value, the loan, the DSCR, the return. And it is the number most commonly flattered in a sale package: management fees understated or omitted, maintenance normalized to an unusually gentle year, vacancy assumed at zero, utility costs from before the last rate increase.

The underwriter's habit is normalization: rebuilding the income statement line by line to what the building will actually earn and cost under *your* ownership, not the seller's best year. The gap between advertised NOI and normalized NOI is frequently the difference between a sound acquisition and an expensive lesson — on a building valued as a multiple of income, every dollar of overstated NOI can inflate the price by twenty.

#### Cap rate as the price of risk

The capitalization rate is the market's exchange rate between income and value: NOI divided by price. A building earning $142,500 at a 4.75 percent cap rate is a $3,000,000 building.

What a cap rate is *not* is a score. A lower cap rate is not a worse deal, nor a higher one automatically a bargain — the cap rate is the market's pricing of risk, durability, and growth for that income stream, in that market, at that moment. The same physical building commands a different cap rate in Kingston than in midtown Toronto because the market prices its income differently. The investor's task is not to hunt the highest number but to judge whether the risk the cap rate implies is actually present — and Chapter 2's argument, in cap-rate language, is that secondary Ontario markets have offered more yield than their fundamentals demand.

One more relationship completes the picture: the spread between the cap rate and the cost of debt. When a building's cap rate exceeds its interest rate, leverage adds to the equity return — positive leverage, the quiet engine behind every illustration in this guide. When the spread inverts, leverage subtracts. Watching that spread is the single most useful habit a financing-literate investor can build, and Chapter 7's modeling tool makes it visible.

#### Loss-to-lease: the embedded upside

Ontario's rent-regulation structure creates a phenomenon nearly unique to this province's investment math: buildings whose in-place rents sit far below market, held there across long tenancies, with the gap closing primarily on turnover.

That gap — loss-to-lease — is measurable, purchasable upside. A building whose units rent at $1,100 in a $1,500 market carries embedded income growth that no new construction can offer, because a new building leases at market from day one. Underwriting loss-to-lease honestly means estimating turnover conservatively and treating the regulatory framework as the operating fact it is — not as an obstacle, and never as something to be engineered around. The long-hold investor does not need aggressive turnover assumptions; time closes the gap on its own schedule, and the patient owner is the one positioned to receive it.

#### The upside beyond the rent roll

Rent is not the only line an operator improves. Utility optimization — submetering, retrofits, consumption billing where the framework permits — moves expenses that older buildings carry by inertia. Operational professionalization moves the vacancy and collection lines. And beneath some buildings sits the largest lever of all: land whose zoning permits more than what stands on it today, an inheritance of Ontario's intensification-era planning reforms. None of these belongs in the purchase underwriting at full value; all of them belong in the long-hold thesis.

#### What full diligence involves

Everything in this chapter is the *thinking*. The *doing* — the document-by-document, system-by-system verification of a building before closing — runs considerably deeper: title and survey, environmental assessment, physical and mechanical condition, tenancy files and estoppels, financial records, tax and utility histories, insurance, and the regulatory record of the building itself. McKinney Realty clients work through this with our Master Diligence Checklist, the full diligence framework we maintain for engaged purchasers. This guide's role is to ensure that by the time you hold that document, nothing in it surprises you.

### Chapter Five — Structure Before You Sign

#### The decision you make once

Of every decision in an acquisition, ownership structure is the one that is easiest to defer and most expensive to revisit. How you hold the building — personally, through a corporation, alongside partners — is settled at closing, and restructuring afterward generally means triggering the very taxes the structure existed to manage.

The uncomfortable truth this chapter exists to deliver: the structure question must be answered *before your first offer*, with your accountant, because the right answer depends on facts only you have — your income, your existing corporations, your family, your horizon. What follows is the map, not the advice.

#### Personal, corporate, and in between

At altitude, three broad configurations cover most first acquisitions. **Personal ownership** is the simplest: income taxed at your marginal rate, direct access to losses, no corporate maintenance — and no separation between the building's liabilities and your own balance sheet. **A holding corporation** introduces that separation, enables income to be retained and taxed at corporate rates, and — decisive for the theme of this guide — creates a share structure that can be reorganized, frozen, and transferred across a generation in ways personally held real estate cannot. **Professionals with existing corporations** face a third dimension: whether and how retained earnings already sitting at corporate rates can fund the acquisition, and what passive-income rules mean for the operating business alongside it.

Each configuration carries trade-offs in tax rate, liability, financing (lenders underwrite the structure too), and succession. The pattern worth internalizing: the more generational your intent, the more the corporate configurations earn their complexity.

#### The CCA machine

Canadian tax law permits the owner of an income property to deduct capital cost allowance — depreciation — against the building's income each year. For many buildings, CCA shelters most or all of the property's taxable income throughout the hold: the cash flows, and the tax largely waits.

It waits; it does not disappear. On sale, CCA previously claimed is *recaptured* — brought back into income in the year of disposition, alongside the capital gain on the building's appreciation. A building held twenty years with CCA claimed throughout can carry a recapture liability in the hundreds of thousands of dollars, arriving all at once. This is not a flaw in the plan; it is the plan — decades of deferral have real value, and the liability is knowable in advance. But it is why the exit must be understood at the entrance, and why the disciplined owner tracks the building's tax position as carefully as its rent roll. Volume II of this series treats the exit side of this machine in full.

#### No 1031 north of the border

American investors enjoy a mechanism — the Section 1031 like-kind exchange — that lets them sell one building, buy another, and defer the tax indefinitely along the way. Canada has no equivalent. A sale here is a taxable event, full stop: capital gain and CCA recapture both crystallize.

This single difference reshapes Canadian strategy in two ways. First, it raises the value of *holding*: where an American might trade buildings the way a portfolio manager trades positions, the Canadian math rewards buying well and staying — which is precisely the refinance-and-hold discipline Chapter 7 describes. Second, it raises the stakes of structure: with no exchange mechanism to fall back on, the corporate reorganizations, rollovers, and estate mechanisms available under Canadian law are the levers that remain, and they only work if the ownership structure was built to permit them. The questions to put to your accountant before your first offer begin here.

### Chapter Six — The Path to a First Acquisition

#### The process at one-page altitude

A disciplined acquisition moves through six phases.

**Before the Search** — mandate, structure, financing pre-positioning, and criteria, settled before a single listing is viewed. **Sourcing** — on-market screening and, more decisively, off-market origination. **Underwriting & Offer** — the Chapter 4 discipline applied to a live building, priced, and negotiated. **Diligence** — the full verification framework, conditions managed on a clock. **Financing** — the insured-lending process of Chapter 3 executed in parallel, on its own considerable timeline. **Closing** — where legal, lender, and operational threads converge into keys and a rent roll that is now yours.

That is the whole process in six sentences, and six sentences is exactly the altitude this guide maintains. Clients of McKinney Realty receive our complete Acquisition Process guide — the phase-by-phase companion built for people actually in the deal — at engagement.

#### The team

No serious acquisition is a solo act, and assembling the team belongs to the first phase, not the last. The working roster: an **advisory broker** who leads sourcing, underwriting, negotiation, and process; a **commercial mortgage specialist** fluent in CMHC timelines; an **accountant** engaged before the first offer, for the reasons Chapter 5 made plain; a **real estate lawyer** with genuine multi-residential experience; a **property manager** consulted during diligence rather than hired after closing, because operating assumptions belong in the underwriting; and the **inspection and environmental professionals** the diligence phase requires. The pattern across all six: engaged early, each one converts risk into information.

#### What principal-led advisory means

The 4-to-40-unit segment described in Chapter 2 is underserved for a structural reason: it is too small for institutional brokerage economics and too specialized for residential practice. McKinney Realty exists in that gap deliberately.

Principal-led means the two Brokers whose names are on the firm are in every deal — sourcing it, underwriting it, negotiating it — not supervising juniors from a distance. In practice that carries three advantages for an acquiring client: access to off-market opportunities surfaced through relationships built across four decades in these markets; underwriting on every opportunity presented, in the Chapter 4 discipline, before it reaches your desk; and a single accountable relationship from mandate through closing — and into the ownership years beyond it, where the next chapter begins.

### Chapter Seven — Building Across Generations

#### The second building is easier than the first

Everything to this point describes one acquisition. The generational outcomes belong to owners who repeat it — and the mechanism of repetition is already inside the first building.

As the engines of Chapter 1 run — principal retired, income grown, value compounded — the building's equity swells and its leverage quietly falls. A refinancing resets the equation: the owner borrows against the building's *current* value, returns a portion of the accumulated equity to hand — without selling, and therefore without triggering the taxes Chapter 5 described — and deploys it as the down payment on the next building. The first building continues operating, now servicing a right-sized loan; the second begins its own compounding run.

This — refinance and hold, never sell — is the discipline behind most of the quiet multi-family fortunes in this province. It is the Canadian answer to the missing 1031: the tax never crystallizes because the sale never happens. It demands conservatism at every refinancing, because leverage restored is risk restored. Done patiently, it converts one disciplined acquisition into a portfolio, one decade at a time.

#### The long arc

Return, one final time, to the building from Chapter 1 — $3,000,000, fifteen units, $750,000 of equity — and let the same ordinary assumptions run for twenty years. Nothing exotic: income growing 2.5 percent annually, the market's pricing unchanged, the mortgage amortizing on schedule.

Two decades on, the illustration looks like this: the building's value has grown toward $4.9 million; the loan has amortized below $1.1 million; the owner's equity approaches $4 million before counting a single year's accumulated cash flow. Call it roughly five times the original investment — from one building, in a version of events where nothing interesting ever happened. (Illustrative — assumptions as stated; actual results vary and are not assured.)

Now add the discipline of the previous section: a refinancing in year eight or nine funds a second building, that building's growth funds a third, and the arithmetic stops being about one asset and becomes about a system. This is how ordinary annual numbers become extraordinary generational ones — not through any single spectacular year, but through engines left running across decades and equity that is redeployed instead of consumed. The multiples such a system can illustrate over twenty-five or thirty years are large enough that this guide declines to print them as prose; they are better explored honestly, with your own assumptions, in the tool below.

### Chapter One — What You've Built

#### The quiet asset

Somewhere in your files is a closing statement with a date on it — fifteen, twenty-five, maybe forty years ago — recording the day you bought a building that has since done something remarkable, mostly without ceremony. It collected rent. It paid down its own mortgage. It sheltered its income while its value compounded. It may now be worth several times what you paid for it, and it almost certainly represents one of the largest positions on your family's balance sheet.

Buildings like yours rarely make headlines, and their owners rarely advertise. But the arithmetic is not a secret to everyone: the unsolicited calls, the letters from brokers, the "buyer with strong interest in your area" — you have likely had them for years, and they have likely gotten more frequent. There is a reason for that, and it is worth understanding on your own terms rather than a caller's.

This guide exists to put a framework around the decisions that eventually arrive with a long-held building: whether to keep holding, whether to refinance, whether to sell, and how — in any of those cases — the asset moves to the next generation without being diminished in transit. It does not conclude that you should sell. It does not conclude that you should hold. It concludes that owners who understand their position make better decisions than owners who respond to phone calls — and it is written to move you firmly into the first group.

#### Why the calls keep coming

Understand what you own in today's terms. A stabilized, income-producing apartment building in an Ontario market has become a peculiar kind of asset: expensive to replicate and increasingly rare in prospect. The past two years delivered record purpose-built rental construction across the country — and the pipeline behind that surge is now visibly thinning, with national housing starts projected to decline each year through 2028 as construction costs, development charges, and financing conditions squeeze project feasibility. New buildings must lease at today's costs; yours operates at yesterday's basis. Analysts covering the Ontario market expect new supply to fall off materially within two to three years.

Institutional capital has noticed. Institutions deployed nearly $15 billion into Canadian real estate in 2025 — roughly a third of the entire investment market and their largest share of acquisitions since 2021 — and multi-family investment outpaced every other asset class in the final quarter of the year. Transaction activity in the GTA opened 2026 up sharply from the year before. None of this obligates you to do anything. All of it explains your phone.

#### What this guide covers

Chapter 2 lays out the honest economics of continuing to hold — costs and value, both columns, no thumb on the scale. Chapter 3 presents the framework at the centre of this guide: hold, refinance, or sell as a structured decision rather than a reaction, with an interactive tool for the arithmetic. Chapter 4 explains why your tax position — not your asking price — is the first number that matters, and why that conversation precedes any listing conversation by months or years. Chapter 5 addresses succession: how a building crosses a generation without a forced sale. Chapter 6 covers what a properly run disposition involves, for the owner who chooses one. Chapter 7 closes where our own family's story does — with what stewardship across generations actually looks like.

You have already done the hard part. You bought well, or held long enough that it no longer matters, and you let the asset work. What remains is to finish the job with the same discipline it started with.

### Chapter Two — The Economics of Holding

#### Both columns, honestly

Any adviser who tells a long-hold owner that the decision is obvious — in either direction — is selling something. The truthful version is a ledger with two columns, and it is worth writing both of them down without flinching.

#### The cost column

**Operating costs have outpaced regulated revenue.** Insurance has been among the fastest-escalating lines in multi-residential operating statements for years. Utilities, maintenance labour, and materials have all repriced. Meanwhile, for buildings first occupied before November 2018, revenue growth on sitting tenancies is bounded by Ontario's annual guideline — with above-guideline increases available only through a formal application process with its own costs, timelines, and limits. The squeeze between market-priced expenses and regulated revenue is real, and it compounds.

**The capital-needs curve bends upward.** Buildings age on a schedule that does not consult their owners. Roofs, boilers, windows, balconies, electrical services — and, for mid-rise stock, elevators, where modernization is routinely a six-figure event. A building held for decades is often approaching several of these at once. The honest question is not whether the building has been maintained — it is what the next ten years of capital work costs, and who should be the one to fund it.

**The regulatory workload is heavier than it was.** Landlord and Tenant Board timelines, documentation standards, and compliance obligations have made self-management more demanding than the era in which many long-hold owners learned the trade. For some families this is a manageable evolution; for others it is the quiet reason ownership has stopped being satisfying.

**The near-term market is softer than the headlines of three years ago.** Record recent completions pushed national purpose-built vacancy to 3.1 percent — up from 2.2 percent a year earlier — and rent growth has moderated, with CMHC expecting further easing in the near term as new units absorb. An owner underwriting the next few years should do so with those facts, not with 2022's.

#### The value column

**Scarcity is moving in your favour.** The same data has a second half: the construction pipeline behind those record completions is contracting, with national starts projected to decline each year through 2028 and analysts expecting Ontario's new supply to fall off materially within two to three years. Stabilized existing stock — your column of the ledger — becomes relatively scarcer as that happens. The near-term soft patch and the medium-term scarcity are both true. They are about different years.

**Your building carries embedded upside no new building has.** Long tenancies at below-market rents are, viewed as an investor views them, purchasable future income — loss-to-lease, in underwriting language. Time closes that gap on its own schedule. The patient holder is positioned to receive it; equally, it is a significant part of what a buyer is paying for, which matters in Chapter 6.

**Refinance capacity is stored liquidity.** Decades of amortization and appreciation mean the building can likely return substantial capital to your hands through refinancing — without a sale, and therefore without a taxable event. Chapter 3 treats this as the underused middle option it is.

**The income is durable.** People need housing in every part of the cycle. Whatever else is true of the cost column, few assets your family could own produce income with this reliability, secured by something this tangible.

#### What the ledger is for

The ledger does not produce a verdict; it produces clarity about which column is growing faster *for your specific building and your specific family*. A well-maintained building with modest coming capex, engaged successors, and comfortable financing reads one way. A building facing an elevator, a roof, and a generation with no interest in operating it reads another. The next chapter turns that reading into a decision framework.

### Chapter Three — Hold, Refinance, or Sell: A Framework

#### Three legitimate answers

Most owners are only ever presented with two options, by people who earn a fee on exactly one of them. There are three, and every one of them is, in the right circumstances, the correct answer.

**Holding** is the default that built the asset, and it remains correct when the income still serves its purpose, the family still wants the work, and the capital-needs curve is fundable from operations. Nothing in this guide argues with forty years of evidence that patient holding works.

**Refinancing** is the under-discussed middle. A building with decades of accumulated equity can return a substantial portion of it through a new financing — capital to hand, no sale, no taxable event — while the building continues operating. Families use refinance proceeds to fund capital work without draining savings, to equalize among children where one will operate and others will not, or to diversify a concentrated balance sheet. It restores leverage, and leverage restored is risk restored — the same conservatism that governed the purchase must govern the refinancing. But for the owner whose reservations are about liquidity or concentration rather than about ownership itself, this is frequently the answer that fits.

And refinancing has a second form worth naming on its own: **refinance and grow.** The equity in a long-held building is not only stored liquidity — it is a down payment on the next one. An owner who refinances and acquires again is running the exact discipline that built most of this province's quiet multi-family fortunes, described from the buyer's side in Volume I of this series: equity redeployed instead of consumed, the tax never crystallizing because the sale never happens. For families with engaged successors, it is often the most elegant succession move available — the next generation cuts its teeth operating a building purchased with the first building's equity, learning the trade on an asset the family controls rather than inheriting a decision they never practiced for. Growth is not every steward's answer; an owner already stretched by one building's capital needs should not borrow into two. But for the family whose hesitation is "we're not done building" rather than "we're done owning," this is the option the cold-callers will never mention — because there is no listing in it.

**Selling** is correct when the honest ledger says so: when the coming capital program belongs to the next owner, when no successor wants the operation, when the family's capital has a genuinely better home, when concentration risk has become uncomfortable, or when the owner is simply done — a reason that requires no further justification after decades of stewardship. A sale executed deliberately, with the tax position engineered in advance and the asset positioned properly, is not the end of stewardship. It is its final act, done well or done poorly.

#### The questions that sort them

Five questions do most of the sorting work. What is the capital *for* — income, a project, the next generation, peace of mind? Who operates the building in ten years — and do they want to? What does the next decade of capital work cost, and who should fund it? What is the tax position today — and what would it be after the structuring moves of Chapters 4 and 5? And what does the arithmetic actually say — not felt, but computed?

The first four are conversations. The fifth is a calculation, and it should be done before any of the conversations harden into positions.

### Chapter Four — Your Tax Position Comes First

#### The number on the cheque

Every owner knows their building's approximate value. Very few know their approximate net proceeds — and the distance between those two numbers is where most disposition planning fails before it begins.

A sale price is a headline. The cheque is what remains after a stack of deductions that has been quietly assembling for as long as you have owned the building: the mortgage discharge, including any prepayment penalty if the sale lands mid-term; transaction costs; and then the tax components — the recapture of every dollar of capital cost allowance claimed across the hold, and the capital gain on the appreciation itself, both crystallizing in a single tax year. For a building held for decades with CCA claimed throughout, the combined liability is routinely the largest single line in the stack, and it arrives all at once.

[Panel: From Price to Cheque — The Stack]
- Sale price — The headline — the last number in this stack you should form a view on
- Mortgage discharge — Outstanding balance, plus any prepayment penalty if the sale lands mid-term
- Transaction costs — Advisory, legal, and closing costs
- CCA recapture — Every dollar of capital cost allowance claimed across the hold, returned to income at once
- Tax on the gain — The appreciation itself, crystallizing in the same tax year
- Net proceeds — The cheque — the number every decision in this guide should be priced against

None of this is a reason not to sell. Decades of tax deferral had real value — that was the plan working, not failing. It is a reason to know the number *before* forming a view on price, because owners anchor on the headline and are ambushed by the cheque, and ambushed owners make poor decisions late in a process when their leverage is lowest.

#### The sequence most owners get backwards

The conventional sequence is: decide to sell, call a broker, list, and let the accountant sort out the taxes at filing time. That sequence forfeits money, and sometimes a great deal of it, because the most valuable tax planning happens *before* a transaction exists.

How the building is held determines what is possible. A personally held building and a corporately held one face different mechanics at sale. A corporately held building opens a question that a personal holding cannot: whether the transaction is a sale of the *asset* or a sale of the *shares* — structures with genuinely different tax outcomes for the seller, different risk profiles for the buyer, and therefore different prices at which each side should rationally transact. Owners who learn this vocabulary before negotiating have options; owners who learn it during diligence have regrets.

And Canada offers no escape hatch on a plain sale. American owners can defer through a like-kind exchange; a Canadian sale is a taxable event, full stop. What Canadian law offers instead are structuring tools — corporate rollovers, reorganizations, and the estate mechanisms of the next chapter — and nearly all of them share one property: they work when arranged in advance, and poorly or not at all when attempted mid-transaction.

#### The two-conversation rule

The practical discipline is simple: the accountant conversation precedes the broker conversation, ideally by a year or more. Bring your accountant the building's cost base, the CCA history, the corporate structure if there is one, and the honest range of family intentions — hold, refinance, sell, transfer. Ask what each path costs in tax as things stand, and what advance structuring would change. Then, and only then, is a conversation about market value standing on solid ground.

We hold this position knowing it delays our own involvement, and we hold it anyway, because the alternative produces worse outcomes for owners. When McKinney Realty conducts a pre-sale consultation, the tax position is where it starts — not because we advise on tax, which we do not, but because no honest disposition strategy can be built without it.

### Chapter Five — Succession Without a Forced Sale

#### The transfer is the point

If generational wealth means anything, it means the asset outlives its first owner without being broken on the way through. Yet that passage — not vacancy, not interest rates — is where long-held buildings are most often lost, because tax law treats death as a disposition: absent planning, the building is deemed sold at fair market value, the accumulated gain and recapture crystallize, and the estate must find the tax from somewhere. When "somewhere" turns out to be a hurried sale of the building itself, decades of stewardship end at the worst table an owner's family will ever negotiate at: a deadline, a liability, and a buyer who knows both.

Planned succession exists to make sure that table is never set.

#### The vocabulary of a planned transfer

What follows is a map of the standard mechanisms — awareness-level, deliberately so. Each is a question for your accountant and your lawyer, and the right combination depends entirely on your family's facts.

**The estate freeze.** The cornerstone concept of Canadian succession planning: the current owner's interest is fixed — frozen — at today's value, while future growth accrues to the next generation, typically through new shares. The founder's eventual tax liability becomes a known, plannable quantity instead of an open-ended one that grows with every year of appreciation; the growth from freeze-day forward is taxed a generation later.

**The Section 85 rollover.** The mechanism that moves a personally held building into a corporation without triggering immediate tax — frequently the enabling first step, because most of the flexible succession structures require corporate ownership to exist at all. This is also why Volume I of this series urges buyers to settle structure before their first offer: options at the end of a hold are largely determined at its beginning. If your building is personally held today, this is the first question to bring to your accountant — the answer shapes everything after it.

**Family trusts and the 21-year rule.** Trusts can hold growth shares, spread outcomes across children and grandchildren, and postpone hard allocation decisions. They come with their own clock: Canadian tax law deems a trust to dispose of its assets every 21 years, a horizon that must be planned for from the day the trust is settled, not discovered in year twenty.

**Insurance as liquidity.** Where a terminal tax liability cannot be structured away, families frequently fund it in advance — life insurance sized to the projected liability, so the estate pays the tax without selling the asset. Not a tax strategy; a liquidity strategy. Often the difference between a building that stays and a building that goes.

#### The part the professionals cannot do

The mechanisms are the lawyers' and accountants' craft. What only the family can supply is the governance: who operates the building, who merely benefits from it, and how the two are kept from becoming the same argument every Thanksgiving. The most elegant freeze fails if no one wants to run the asset, and a modest structure succeeds if the operating question was answered honestly. Some families answer it by growing: a second building, purchased with the first one's refinanced equity and operated by the successor, turns succession from a paperwork event into an apprenticeship — the next generation proves the operating answer years before the transfer depends on it. In our experience the families who transfer buildings well are not the ones with the most sophisticated structures — they are the ones who had the operating conversation early, out loud, and revisited it as children became adults.

Succession planning is where holding and selling stop being opposites: a family that has built the structure to hold across a generation has also, necessarily, built the clarity to sell well if it ever chooses to. The next chapter is for the owner who does.

### Chapter Six — If You Sell: Maximizing the Outcome

#### Deliberate, not reactive

There are two kinds of dispositions: the ones that begin with a plan, and the ones that begin with a phone call. The entire difference in outcome — and it is routinely a difference measured in six figures — lies in which one yours is.

A reactive sale accepts a buyer's timing, a buyer's information advantage, and a buyer's framing of value. A deliberate sale reverses all three. Everything in this chapter is about that reversal, at the level of principle; the operational companion — our Disposition Readiness Checklist and the full disposition process — is the client deliverable McKinney Realty brings to an engagement, and this guide will not counterfeit it in summary form.

#### Preparation is pricing

By the time a building is on the market, most of its price has already been determined — by the state of its records. Multi-family assets are bought on their numbers, and numbers that cannot be verified are numbers a buyer discounts. Clean financial statements that reconcile; a current, accurate rent roll; organized tenancy files; utility and tax histories; documentation of capital work — these are not administrative niceties. They are the difference between a buyer underwriting your building's actual income and a buyer underwriting their own skepticism.

The same is true of the physical asset, with a discipline worth stating plainly: the goal is not renovation, it is the removal of excuses. Deferred items that are cheap to fix and expensive to negotiate — the leak stain, the dead intercom, the mechanical room that photographs like a warning — get resolved. Major capital items get honest engineering assessments, because a priced, documented liability negotiates better than a discovered one. Buyers pay for certainty. Preparation manufactures it.

#### Positioning is the second price

The building sold as a listing and the building sold as an investment are the same building at different prices. An investment-grade process presents an underwritten package — normalized operating statement, documented upside, the loss-to-lease story told with evidence — to a curated universe of qualified purchasers, and runs a structured process that lets competition, not a single caller's opening number, discover the price. Your decades of below-market tenancies, properly documented, are a purchasable asset to the right buyer; unexplained, they are a reason to bid low.

Discretion deserves its own word. Many owners have excellent reasons — tenants, staff, family, competitors — to prefer that a sale not be public. An off-market process, run properly against a known buyer pool, trades some breadth for confidentiality while preserving competitive tension. It is a legitimate strategy, not a lesser one, and the current market has depth enough to support it: transaction activity has returned decisively, with GTA multi-family volume opening 2026 sharply above the prior year. That is stated as a fact about liquidity, not an argument about timing — the framework of Chapter 3, not the market's mood, decides whether you sell.

#### The seat we take

In a disposition engagement, McKinney Realty's role is the one this guide has been describing throughout: the tax conversation first, the readiness work before the market ever hears the building's address, the underwritten positioning, the curated process — on-market or off — and principal-led negotiation through to close. Two Brokers, one accountable relationship, and a standard set by the fact that we advise sellers the way our own family would expect to be advised. ### Chapter Seven — Stewardship Across Generations

#### What a held portfolio becomes

Volume I of this series ends with an arithmetic illustration: one building, ordinary assumptions, twenty patient years, and equity several times the original investment. This volume can end with something better than an illustration.

The original McKinney Realty was founded by the family's first Broker — Sean's father, Liam's grandfather — who, alongside the brokerage, quietly practiced everything these two guides describe: he bought an apartment building, held it, refinanced it, and bought the next, until one building had become a portfolio. The portfolio was held. It crossed a generation. Sean carried the practice forward, purchased the local RE/MAX franchise, and grew it into the 75-plus-agent brokerage it is today. Liam is the third generation.

We advise on this work because our family has lived it — slowly, unevenly, and across three generations, which is how compounding actually happens. We have sat on your side of the decisions in this guide: the capex years, the refinancing conversations, the succession questions asked around an actual kitchen table. Reviving the McKinney Realty name for this practice was not nostalgia; it was accuracy. And it is why we bring a particular seriousness to every owner's building: we know first-hand what one well-held property can become — and what it takes to keep it whole on the way through.

#### The steward's definition of success

Success, for the steward, is not a number. It is optionality preserved: a building whose records would survive diligence tomorrow, a tax position known rather than feared, a structure that permits transfer without forcing sale, a family that has had the operating conversation, and a decision — hold, refinance, sell, or pass on — made from the framework of Chapter 3 rather than from a stranger's phone call. An owner in that position wins on every path, including the paths not taken.

Getting there is not a transaction. It is a sequence of unhurried conversations — accountant, lawyer, family, and an advisor who understands the asset class — most of which cost nothing but candour, and all of which are worth more the earlier they happen.

If this volume has done its work, you now hold a ledger, a framework, and a vocabulary — and the next step is not a listing agreement. It is a conversation about your building, your structure, and your family's intentions, with no conclusion presumed.
