## id 3 — Course Day 0 (Lesson 1 of 8)
**Subject:** A condo is worth what your neighbour got. A building is worth what it earns.
**In-body title:** Two ways to price real estate
There are two ways to price real estate.
The first: a property is worth what the last comparable one sold for. That's houses and condos — value that floats on other buyers' optimism, with nothing underneath when sentiment leaves.
The second: a property is worth what it earns. That's apartment buildings — value that stands on a rent roll. Income divided by yield. A floor under the price.
One consequence of the second way changes everything: if value is built on income, then an owner who can raise the income can raise the value. That lever exists nowhere else in residential real estate.

## id 4 — Course Day 2 (Lesson 2 of 8)
**Subject:** Four furnaces, four renewals, one part-time job
**In-body title:** Why buildings beat doors
That's what four rental houses actually are: four roofs, four insurance policies, four tax bills, four renewal dates — and a lending wall, because most A-lenders cap out at four or five rental mortgages per borrower no matter how well you've invested.
A building consolidates all of it: one roof, one policy, one renewal — and a financing framework with no door-count cap, because it underwrites the building's income instead of your paycheque.
Lesson 2 walks the real math, including the part most people selling buildings won't say out loud: a building means MORE tenancies, not fewer — what changes is how much each incident costs you and who handles it.

## id 5 — Course Day 4 (Lesson 3 of 8)
**Subject:** The five numbers that price any building in the country
**In-body title:** Reading a building like a buyer
Rent roll minus vacancy minus honest expenses = NOI. NOI ÷ asking price = the cap rate the seller is really asking you to pay. That's the entire literacy — two documents, five numbers, back of an envelope.
Lesson 3 teaches you to read both documents the way buyers do, including where sellers hide the soft spots: the suspiciously low maintenance line (deferred, not absent), the missing management fee, the 25% expense ratio that's a story rather than a building.
Homework included: pull a real Ontario listing and price it yourself. First time takes twenty minutes. Tenth time takes four.

## id 6 — Course Day 6 (Lesson 4 of 8)
**Subject:** The bank stops at property five. CMHC starts at unit five.
**In-body title:** The financing nobody tells beginners about
Residential lending caps out — most A-lenders won't hold more than four or five rental mortgages per borrower, because your salary can only stretch so far.
At five units, a property crosses into CMHC's multi-family framework, and the question flips: not "can your income carry this debt?" but "can the building's income carry this debt?" The building qualifies, not your T4. Insured rates run cheaper than conventional, amortizations run longer, and there's no cap on how many buildings qualify themselves.
The reach math surprises people: a six-unit in a secondary market can require less equity than most assume — Lesson 4 works the illustration, plus the two honest cautions (process and premiums).

## id 7 — Course Day 8 (Lesson 5 of 8)
**Subject:** The tax bill nobody mentions when they tell you to scale up
**In-body title:** The honest costs ← RETITLE (see change table)
Canada has no 1031 exchange. If your path to a building runs through selling rentals you already own, every sale is a taxable event — half the gain lands on your income that year, and if you've been claiming CCA, the recapture comes back as fully taxable income on top.
Add land transfer tax (doubled in Toronto), the CMHC premium, and closing costs, and the toll booth between scattered doors and one building is real money.
Lesson 5 puts every toll on the table — and then does the thing courses like this usually skip: names the situations where buying a building is the wrong move. You should hear that from the people who'd like to work with you someday.

## id 8 — Course Day 11 (Lesson 6 of 8)
**Subject:** The move that buys building #2 without selling building #1
**In-body title:** The compounding mechanic
Three mechanics, none complicated:
Tenants retire the debt — part of every rent cheque is mortgage principal, equity you never had to save. Income growth compounds into value — because buildings are priced on income, every dollar of new annual rent is roughly twenty dollars of building value. And refinancing recycles the equity — pull your capital back out against the grown value, without selling, and buy building two.
Repeat. That's the entire strategy. No timing, no exit, no greater fool required.
Lesson 6 walks the full cycle with numbers — and ends with homework that matters: run YOUR numbers through our free Compounding Modeler and reply with the 25-year figure you get. A person reads these, and that conversation is usually the best part of this course.

## id 9 — Course Day 13 (Lesson 7 of 8)
**Subject:** $50/month of rent = $144,000 of value. Here's why.
**In-body title:** The levers
Raise rents $50 a month across twelve units: $7,200 a year. Now price it the way buildings are priced — divide by a 5% cap rate — and it's $144,000 of building value. From fifty dollars.
That's one of four levers. The others: expenses (every $2,000 of annual waste eliminated is $40,000 of value), the spread between what the building yields and what the debt costs (when it's positive, the borrowed money itself is profitable), and growth (2% vs. 3% rent growth is a rounding error in year one and a different life in year 25).
Owners control these numbers. That's the entire difference between this and every other thing you could do with capital — you can't raise a stock's earnings, and nobody pays down your index fund's debt.
Lesson 7 runs all four levers through the Modeler, then the biggest one: the start date.

## id 10 — Course Day 16 (Lesson 8 of 8)
**Subject:** What a broker actually does — and what it costs you (plainly)
**In-body title:** What working with a broker actually looks like
Six phases from decision to keys: mandate, sourcing (including the off-market buildings that never hit a portal), underwriting, negotiation, due diligence, closing. Months, not weeks — a federal agency is involved, and thoroughness is the point.
What it costs: brokers earn commission on completed deals, and in most purchases it's paid from the seller's side. Talking costs nothing and obliges nothing.
And the honest ending to an honest course: most people finishing it shouldn't buy a building this month. Keep your Modeler scenario alive. Read the Builder's Guide when you want the deeper version. And when your numbers say it's real — this year or in five — we'll still be here.
